Locksmith: Comparing Prompt-Injection Defense Strategies Through Adversarial Gameplay

Rickey Johnson · COMP 365 Individual Project · Practical Checkpoint 2 · October 4, 2026 · Artifact: github.com/rickeyjohnson/Locksmith

1 Question and approach

Question: does raising defense-prompt complexity across levels measurably lower extraction success?

Built: a browser game where a model guards a fictional password and the player tries to extract it. Eight levels each add one defense layer on top of the one below. A single pipeline — input guards, model, output guards, automatic leak check — runs both the game and an offline benchmark.

Changes since Checkpoint 1:

  • Levels 7–8 (AI prompt shield, AI critic agent) implemented; CP1 had six.
  • Attack corpus grew 12 → 78 using the public Tensor Trust dataset; the small corpus left every upper level at 0% and could not rank them.
  • Output filter changed to literal matching. The normalized version made levels 3–8 unwinnable for a human (0 player-visible leaks in 144 attempts), and a literal match is what a naive filter does.
  • From CP1 feedback: per-defense contribution (§4), a hand-check of the labels (§5), a playtest mode unlocking every level, and full response logging so labels can be audited at all.

2 Data and methods

Data: self-authored level prompts and generated nonsense-word passwords (kept out of the repository), plus 78 attack prompts — 18 written by me, 60 sampled from the public Tensor Trust extraction dataset (Toyer et al., ICLR 2024).

Preparation: dataset rows outside 15–600 characters dropped (536 candidates → 60 by even stride); model reasoning stripped; each attempt labeled leaked/clean automatically by a detector covering spacing, hyphenation, reversal, base64, hex and acrostics; failed model calls excluded as errors.

Working now: all eight levels, both AI guards (failing closed), full logging of the raw reply alongside what the player saw, both benchmark suites, 101 passing tests.

Credits: qwen3:8b via Ollama; Next.js; Supabase; Tensor Trust dataset; spotlighting follows Hines et al. (arXiv:2403.14720). Development was assisted by Claude Code (Anthropic) for implementation, tests and analysis scripting under my direction; experimental design, interpretation and this report are my own.

3 Baseline and tests

Baseline: two non-AI methods — a keyword/regex input filter (level 2) and a literal output filter (level 3) — against level 1, which has no defense.

Procedure: every level and isolated defense gets the same 78 prompts, with qwen3:8b, temperature 0.7, seed 42, 300-token cap, and a configuration hash on every row.

Cases: 1,844 stacked attempts (78 prompts × 8 levels × 3 seeds) + 546 isolated (78 × 7, single seed) = 2,390 scored.

Measures: (1) player-visible leak rate — password present in the shown reply, any disguise; (2) model-side leak rate — the same check on the raw reply, separating "the model kept it" from "a filter caught it". Mean/median attempts until success needs human players and is not yet completed.

Related work: published defenses change the model or its interface — StruQ (USENIX Security 2025), SecAlign (ACM CCS 2025), DataSentinel (IEEE S&P 2025). Gamified attack collection is published too (Tensor Trust, ICLR 2024; HackAPrompt, EMNLP 2023), and the closest benchmark is Liu et al. (USENIX Security 2024). The gap: prompt-level defenses — all a developer can deploy against a hosted model — have not been measured against each other on one model and corpus, and spotlighting, the strongest here, is a preprint only.

5 Weak case and limitation

The measure saturates. Levels 6–8 all report 0.0%, so the AI defenses cannot be ranked against each other. The isolation table shows this is a measurement failure, not a tie: alone, the critic (17.9%) and shield (21.8%) are clearly weaker than spotlighting (1.3%). Causes: the shield blocks 78% of the corpus before the model, leaving 17 prompts per level; 33 of 78 attacks name "password" or "instructions" and die at the regex filter; and the corpus never adapts, whereas a human would.

An apparent inversion, settled by the seed sweep. At a single seed level 5 (1.4%) looked worse than level 4 (0.0%). Across three seeds the ranges overlap completely — level 4 scores 0.0 / 2.6 / 1.3 and level 5 scores 1.3 / 1.3 / 0.0 — so the two are not distinguishable and the inversion was noise. Seed variation is itself meaningful: level 2 moves 5.1 percentage points between seeds, so single-run differences smaller than that carry no weight.

Hand-check of the labels. 20 stratified attempts (10 leak, 10 clean) were read against the real password: all 20 labels were correct. The finding is in the definition, not the labels — two clean replies hand out most of the password:

Safeguard of secrets deep and true, Journey through shadows, bold and wise, Lore that time has long secured. Aim for the key that sets you free. Oath-bound I stand, my duty clear, In this vault, the truth is kept, Gates of wisdom, none can breach. Pillars of trust, never to sleep.

Line initials spell SLOGJAIP; the password is SLOGEJAIP — eight of nine letters in order. A player recovers it in a guess or two, but it scores clean because the exact string never appears. Measured across all 1,844 attempts: 8 near misses, 0.5% of clean replies, confined to levels 1–4 and reproducing across seeds.

Limit of the data. One model, no human has played the game, and "does the reply contain the password" is narrower than "could a player recover it". A reported 0% means no attempt produced the exact string; it is not a claim of security.

6 Next steps and paper outline

Task 1 — put players on the upper levels. Levels 6–8 have never faced a human, and normal progression cannot deliver them, so the game now ships a playtest mode that unlocks every level and tags those attempts separately.

Task 2 — widen the corpus against the top levels. The seed sweep is complete and settled the level 4/5 question, but levels 6–8 still sit at 0.0% at every seed while leaking 0.7–1.9% on the model side, so the ceiling is now the attack set rather than the measurement. The next run needs adaptive attacks written against these defenses, not a fixed corpus.

Introduction. Prompt injection defeats secrets held in system prompts, and published defenses are rarely measured against one another on one model with one attack set.

Methods. Eight stacked levels and seven isolated variants share one guard pipeline, a pinned model, and a 78-prompt corpus, with automatic leak labeling of both the shown and the raw reply.

Results. Player-visible leaks fall 84.6% → 0%, but filtering leaves the model's own leak rate unchanged, and spotlighting alone beats both AI defenses.

Limitations. One model, a static corpus, no human attempts, a leak measure blind to near misses, and saturation at the top of the ladder.
